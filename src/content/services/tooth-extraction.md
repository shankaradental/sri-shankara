## What it is

Removal of a tooth. It is generally a last resort, considered when a tooth
cannot be restored, when infection keeps returning, or when a wisdom tooth is
impacted and causing problems.

## When it may be recommended

- Decay or fracture too extensive to restore
- Advanced gum disease with significant bone loss
- Impacted wisdom teeth causing pain, infection or damage to the tooth in front
- Retained baby teeth, or crowding as part of an orthodontic plan

## Will it hurt?

The extraction is carried out under local anaesthetic. Once the area is numb
you should feel firm pressure and movement, but not pain. Tell the dentist if
you feel anything sharp — more anaesthetic can be given at any point.

If you are anxious about the procedure, say so when booking. Knowing in advance
lets us allow more time and take it at your pace, which makes more difference
than most people expect.

Discomfort once the anaesthetic wears off is managed with ordinary pain relief
taken as directed on the packet. Most people find day two is the peak and it
eases from there.

## What the appointment involves

Local anaesthetic numbs the area. Simple extractions use instruments to loosen
and lift the tooth out. A surgical extraction — more common for impacted wisdom
teeth — involves a small incision and sometimes sectioning the tooth, with
stitches afterwards.

## Afterwards

Bite firmly on the gauze pad for 30–45 minutes. For the first 24 hours avoid
rinsing vigorously, hot drinks, smoking and straws, all of which can dislodge
the clot. Swelling peaks around day two. Contact the clinic if bleeding does not
settle, or if severe pain begins three to four days later.

## Replacing the tooth

Where a visible or chewing tooth is removed, replacement options include an
implant, a bridge or a partial denture. Leaving a gap can allow neighbouring
teeth to drift. The dentist will explain what applies in your case.
