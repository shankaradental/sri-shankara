## Assessing New Technology in Dental Implant Placement

Dental implants are artificial supports placed into the jaw to hold replacement teeth. Placing an implant requires careful physical positioning so that the replacement tooth can function properly while avoiding critical anatomical structures in the jaw. Over the past decade, robotic systems have emerged as a new approach to assist clinicians during this procedure. However, understanding how closely these systems follow surgical plans requires rigorous evaluation.

A systematic review and meta-analysis brought together data from across the field to measure the accuracy of robot-assisted implant placement. Researchers conducted comprehensive searches of major research databases—including PubMed, Scopus, and Embase—covering publications from January 2015 to September 2025. In total, the review identified 39 clinical studies that included 1,227 patients and 2,066 dental implants.

## How Accuracy Was Measured

To determine how precisely a robotic system places an implant compared to the initial digital plan, researchers look at deviations measured in millimetres and degrees:

- **Coronal deviation:** The horizontal difference at the top or entry point of the implant (near the gumline).
- **Apical deviation:** The difference at the bottom or tip of the implant deep within the bone.
- **Angular deviation:** The difference in the tilt or angle of the implant relative to the planned path.

When data from the 39 studies were combined, the pooled estimates showed an average coronal deviation of 0.68 millimetres (with a 95% confidence interval of 0.62 to 0.74 mm). The average apical deviation was 0.72 millimetres (95% confidence interval: 0.66 to 0.79 mm). The average angular deviation was 1.60 degrees (95% confidence interval: 1.40 to 1.81 degrees). 

A confidence interval indicates the range in which the true average is expected to fall based on the statistical analysis. The review noted that these recorded deviations remained well within clinically accepted limits.

## The Effect of Robotic Autonomy

The review examined whether different clinical factors or equipment designs influenced how accurately implants were placed. One significant factor was the level of autonomy of the robotic system:

- **Haptic-guided systems:** Robotic systems that provide physical resistance or tactile guidance to the clinician's hand during drilling.
- **Task-autonomous and semi-autonomous systems:** Devices designed to carry out specific surgical steps independently or with partial clinician steering.

The researchers found that task-autonomous and semi-autonomous systems achieved greater accuracy than haptic-guided approaches. Higher autonomy levels were linked to increased accuracy across a variety of clinical scenarios.

By contrast, other clinical variables did not significantly change the accuracy outcomes. The edentulous span (the length of the gap where teeth are missing), the location of the implant in the mouth, and the underlying study design did not have a statistically significant effect on placement precision.

Overall, the analysis found that robotic systems consistently achieved higher accuracy than previously reported forms of computer-assisted implant surgery (CAIS). The authors highlighted the potential role of robotic CAIS in managing complex situations, such as full-arch rehabilitations (replacing an entire upper or lower arch of teeth), by enhancing predictability and procedural safety.

## Research Limitations

While the review pooled data from over two thousand implants, it also identified important limitations in the existing body of evidence. The 39 reviewed publications included a mix of randomized trials, non-randomized investigations, and observational studies, evaluated using validated risk-of-bias assessment tools. Furthermore, the available data focused primarily on immediate surgical placement accuracy. The review authors stressed that high-quality randomized controlled trials are still needed to validate long-term clinical outcomes over time.

## What This Means for Patients

For patients considering dental implant therapy, this research demonstrates that robotic technology is capable of placing dental implants with a high degree of physical precision that meets established clinical benchmarks. Robotic systems continue to be evaluated alongside other computer-assisted methods to understand how they can aid treatment planning in complex clinical situations. As research continues, long-term studies will help clarify how this precision translates to implant function and stability over many years.
