## Two-Wheeler Accidents and Facial Trauma

Urban roads have seen a notable increase in electric two-wheeled transport, with e-bikes and e-scooters becoming popular alternatives to conventional petrol-driven motorcycles and scooters. Alongside this shift, healthcare researchers have been investigating how vehicle types relate to road traffic accidents, particularly injuries that affect the face, jaw, and teeth. 

A newly published cross-sectional study evaluated the patterns of dental and craniofacial injuries—meaning physical damage to the head, face, jaws, and teeth—sustained by conventional two-wheeler riders compared to electric two-wheeler riders in an urban setting.

## How the Study Was Conducted

The researchers evaluated 652 riders in an urban environment. This total included 340 individuals using conventional two-wheelers and 312 riders operating electric two-wheelers, specifically e-bikes and e-scooters. 

Participants were interviewed using a structured questionnaire and underwent clinical examinations. The researchers collected data on rider demographics, the type of vehicle used, adherence to protective gear, general riding practices and behavior, details surrounding any accidents, and the specific types of oral and facial injuries sustained.

To analyze the data, researchers used statistical tests including Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests. They also performed a binary logistic regression analysis. This statistical approach allowed the researchers to evaluate the probability of facial injuries while adjusting for several potential influencing factors: vehicle type, helmet compliance, whether the rider was riding for commercial or personal purposes, age group, usual travel speed, and whether the rider had received pre-ride training.

## Differences in Accidents and Facial Injuries

The study identified clear statistical differences between the two rider groups regarding crash involvement and facial injuries:

- **Accident rates:** Electric two-wheeler riders were significantly more likely to report being in a road traffic accident than conventional riders. About 70.8% of e-bike riders had experienced an accident, compared with 47.1% of conventional two-wheeler riders. In statistical terms, this represented a relative risk of 1.51.
- **Commercial vs. personal use:** Within each vehicle category, accident rates were similar regardless of whether the vehicle was used for commercial work or personal travel.
- **Facial and oral injury frequency:** Among those who were involved in accidents, riders of electric two-wheelers suffered facial or dental injuries far more often. Specifically, 72.9% of crash-involved electric riders sustained injuries to the face or mouth, compared to 39.4% of conventional riders. This corresponded to an odds ratio of 4.13, indicating a much higher likelihood of facial trauma following an accident.

## Common Types of Mouth and Jaw Injuries

When looking across all riders who suffered facial or oral trauma, the study tracked the specific forms of injury:

- **Soft-tissue lacerations:** Cuts and tears to the skin, lips, or cheeks were the most common injury, present in 39.3% of affected riders.
- **Chipped or broken teeth:** Fractured teeth were the second most frequent problem, occurring in 21.0% of cases.
- **Facial bone or jaw fractures:** Structural breaks in the jaw or other facial bones were recorded in 14.7% of injured individuals.
- **Tooth avulsion:** Complete dislodgement, where a tooth is entirely knocked out of its socket, occurred in 9.8% of cases.
- **Tooth displacement or luxation:** Injuries where a tooth was loosened, tilted, or pushed out of its normal position within the bone were noted in 7.1% of cases.

While electric two-wheeler riders were more likely overall to sustain facial trauma during an accident, the study found no significant differences between the two vehicle groups in the specific breakdown of these individual injury types.

## Study Limitations

When interpreting these findings, several limitations must be considered. The study used a cross-sectional design, relying partly on questionnaires and self-reported details about past accidents, which can be influenced by participant recall. The data also came from a single urban area, meaning conditions such as local road design and traffic patterns might differ elsewhere. Finally, while the researchers adjusted for factors like speed, training, and helmet use, observational studies can demonstrate associations between vehicle type and injury patterns, but they do not isolate a single direct cause.

## What This Means for Patients

For anyone commuting on two wheels, these findings highlight how frequently dental and facial tissues are affected during traffic accidents. Chipped teeth, cuts, loosened teeth, and jaw fractures represent a substantial portion of accident-related harm. Wearing appropriate protective gear, such as an effective helmet, remains an important precaution. If a road accident does occur, prompt assessment by a dental professional can identify hidden fractures, evaluate nerve health in displaced or broken teeth, and guide appropriate steps to save damaged teeth.
