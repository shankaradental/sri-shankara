## Delayed Tooth Development as a Clue for Rare Growth Disorder

Human physical height growth relies on a complex biological network involving growth hormone and another vital hormone known as insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I). For IGF-I to function effectively throughout the body, it must be available in a free, usable form. A recent scientific case report highlights how a rare genetic variation affecting this system can lead to both short stature and delayed tooth development.

### How Hormones and Enzymes Regulate Growth

In the human bloodstream, most IGF-I does not float freely. Instead, it binds to specific carrier proteins called IGF-binding proteins, primarily IGFBP-3 and IGFBP-5. Together with another protein component known as the acid-labile subunit, these structures form a three-part complex. This bound arrangement serves an important purpose: it protects IGF-I and extends the time it remains active in circulation, which is referred to as its half-life.

However, bound IGF-I cannot act on tissues until it is released. An enzyme called pregnancy-associated plasma protein-A2, or PAPP-A2, plays the critical role of cutting (cleaving) IGFBP-3 and IGFBP-5. This enzymatic cleavage frees active, usable IGF-I so that tissues can grow. 

When a person carries disease-causing (pathogenic) variations in the *PAPPA2* gene, this releasing process becomes impaired. Consequently, the amount of usable IGF-I in the body drops significantly, even though total levels of IGF-I circulating in the blood may remain noticeably high.

### Clinical Findings in a Single Case

The study describes a 12.3-year-old girl from Jordan who presented with short stature along with delayed dental development. Initial laboratory testing revealed a distinct biochemical pattern: her total serum IGF-I levels were markedly elevated, and her IGFBP-3 levels were in the high-normal range.

Doctors performed initial genetic evaluations, including a comparative genomic hybridization microarray and targeted sequencing of genes commonly associated with physical growth failure after birth, such as the *IGF1R* gene. These standard genetic tests returned unremarkable, normal results.

A six-month trial of recombinant human growth hormone was conducted to see if it would accelerate her physical growth. The treatment yielded no measurable increase in growth speed. However, evaluating the true effect of the growth hormone treatment was limited, because the patient already had advanced skeletal maturity—meaning her bones were already close to their final stage of growth—when the trial started.

### Discovery of a Novel Genetic Variant

To investigate further, researchers carried out extended genetic sequencing. This advanced analysis revealed a previously unknown (novel) genetic change: a homozygous *PAPPA2* frameshift variant, designated as c.1223delT (p.L408Rfs*49). A frameshift variant is a genetic mutation where a small alteration in the DNA sequence shifts the reading frame, altering how the protein is constructed.

This finding expands the known genetic spectrum of PAPP-A2 deficiency. The authors of the study note that testing for *PAPPA2* gene alterations should be considered when evaluating children who show short stature combined with consistently high total IGF-I levels and negative tests for other growth-related genes like *IGF1R*. Furthermore, delayed tooth emergence or development may serve as an essential clinical indicator to help distinguish PAPP-A2 deficiency from other medical conditions that cause short stature.

### Study Limitations

It is important to view these findings within the context of the study's design. This report details findings from a single patient, so the observations cannot establish how common these exact dental features are across all individuals with *PAPPA2* mutations. Additionally, because the patient began growth hormone therapy at a time when her skeletal development was already advanced, the study could not definitively determine whether growth hormone therapy might have different effects if started at an earlier stage of bone development.

### What This Means for Patients

Dental development often mirrors broader patterns of physical growth in the body. While delayed tooth emergence in children is most frequently related to routine, non-serious factors, this case report illustrates how dental timing can sometimes offer helpful clues in diagnosing rare genetic conditions. Patients and parents facing unexplained growth concerns should always consult with qualified medical specialists and pediatric dentists for thorough diagnostic evaluations tailored to individual health needs.
