When a tooth develops a deep infection inside its root canals, it can lead to a condition known as apical periodontitis. This term refers to an inflammation or infection affecting the tissues surrounding the tip of the tooth's root. While dental clinicians know that these infections are driven by communities of microscopic organisms, researchers are actively investigating how broader health conditions might influence the types of germs living inside an infected tooth.

A systematic review evaluated whether individuals with diabetes mellitus harbor different root canal bacteria and other microorganisms compared to individuals without diabetes when presenting with apical periodontitis.

## How the Evidence Was Gathered

The review authors conducted an extensive electronic search across six scientific literature databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, the Cochrane Library, and EBSCO. The search captured published clinical studies up to April 9, 2026.

To be included in the review, studies had to involve human clinical research comparing diabetic and non-diabetic individuals with infected root canals. The studies also needed to identify microbial species using laboratory culture techniques (growing microbes in lab dishes) or molecular methods (identifying microbes by their genetic material). The researchers extracted details on each participant's diabetes status, the specific type of tooth infection, the laboratory methods used, the identified organisms, and any quantitative measurements of the microbial load. To judge the quality and rigor of the included papers, the authors evaluated each study using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tool.

Ultimately, 16 clinical studies met the criteria and were included in the final analysis.

## Differences in Root Canal Microbes

Among the 16 studies, the majority relied on molecular identification techniques, while only a small number used traditional culture testing or a combination of culture and molecular tools.

Several studies reported differences between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals regarding which microbes were present and how abundant they were. Specifically, researchers observed differences in the prevalence or relative abundance of several bacterial groups in diabetic patients:

- *Peptostreptococcus* species
- *Porphyromonas gingivalis*
- *Eubacterium infirmum*
- *Enterococcus faecalis*
- *Olsenella*
- *Pseudoramibacter*
- *Fannyhessea*

In addition to these bacteria, a limited number of the reviewed studies reported finding differences involving fungi and archaea (another group of single-celled microscopic organisms distinct from bacteria) inside the infected canals.

## Challenges and Study Limitations

While the review uncovered patterns suggesting that diabetes may alter the microbial environment inside an infected tooth, the authors highlighted several significant limitations across the published evidence.

First, the 16 studies differed substantially in their design. They examined different types of root canal infections, used varying laboratory testing methods, and varied in how thoroughly they recorded and reported the patients' glycemic status (blood sugar control). 

Second, the researchers found that it was impossible to directly compare the quantitative amounts of microbes across the studies. The included papers measured microbial quantities using entirely different standards, including:

- Colony-forming unit (CFU) counts from culture plates
- Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection
- Quantitative PCR (qPCR) bacterial cell counts
- Relative abundance data derived from DNA sequencing

Because these measuring sticks are fundamentally different, the data could not be combined into a single statistical calculation. Finally, the findings demonstrate an association between diabetes and differing microbial profiles, but they do not prove that diabetes directly causes these specific organisms to colonize the root canal.

## What This Means for Patients

This research shows that general systemic health and oral health remain closely connected. If you have diabetes, the mix of bacteria and other microorganisms involved in a root canal infection may differ in certain ways from the mix found in someone without diabetes. 

However, because existing studies used diverse methods and varied in how they measured blood sugar control and microbial levels, scientists are still working to understand the exact nature of these differences. Managing both systemic health conditions and dental concerns remains an important part of overall personal care.
