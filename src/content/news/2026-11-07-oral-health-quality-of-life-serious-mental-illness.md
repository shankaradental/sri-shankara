Oral health plays an essential role in a person's overall well-being, affecting basic day-to-day functions like eating and speaking, as well as psychological comfort and social interactions. Clinicians and researchers often evaluate these broad personal impacts using a measure known as Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL). While it is recognized that individuals living with serious mental illness (SMI) experience health inequalities, their specific oral health quality-of-life challenges remain an important area of ongoing investigation.

To systematically map the existing scientific evidence on this topic, a team of researchers conducted a scoping review. Their goal was to examine OHRQoL among adults with serious mental illness and related psychiatric conditions, identify factors linked to poorer quality of life, and highlight gaps in current scientific literature.

## How the Review Was Conducted

The research team followed established methodology frameworks for scoping reviews (specifically the Arksey and O'Malley framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines). They searched six electronic databases for peer-reviewed primary studies published between January 2000 and April 2026.

From an initial identification of 1,586 records, the researchers included 22 publications representing 21 unique study populations. To analyze the data, they classified the evidence into three distinct groups:

* **Serious mental illness (SMI) populations:** Evaluated in nine studies.
* **Other clinically diagnosed psychiatric populations:** Evaluated in six studies.
* **Symptom-defined or community populations:** Identified in six studies using validated screening tools.

Most of the included studies were observational and cross-sectional in design, meaning they gathered information at a single point in time to identify associations.

## Key Factors Linked to Lower Quality of Life

The review showed that adults living with serious mental illness experience a substantial burden on their oral health-related quality of life, involving functional, psychological, and social dimensions.

When examining the studies focused specifically on adults with SMI, the researchers identified several key factors associated with poorer OHRQoL:

* **Oral disease and treatment needs:** Active dental disease and unmet treatment requirements directly impacted daily function and comfort.
* **Xerostomia:** This is the medical term for dry mouth, a condition that can cause physical discomfort and increase the risk of oral health problems.
* **Psychiatric and psychosocial factors:** Underlying mental health conditions and psychological distress were closely connected to lower perceived oral quality of life.
* **Barriers to dental care:** Difficulty accessing routine or necessary dental services contributed to lower quality-of-life scores.

In addition to the SMI groups, poorer OHRQoL was also associated with mental health burden in other clinically diagnosed psychiatric populations and in community groups screened for mental health symptoms.

## Limitations of the Current Research

The authors of the scoping review noted several important limitations in the existing body of research:

* **Study Design:** Most included research relied on cross-sectional observational designs. These studies can identify associations between mental health and oral quality of life, but they cannot prove direct cause-and-effect relationships.
* **Variability across studies:** The studies showed high heterogeneity, meaning there were wide variations in how psychiatric conditions were defined, how studies were designed, and how OHRQoL was measured.
* **Lack of intervention trials:** Crucially, the review identified no studies that evaluated an intervention specifically designed to improve OHRQoL in people living with serious mental illness.

Because of these gaps, the review authors highlighted the need for clearer definitions of psychiatric populations, as well as future longitudinal studies (tracking participants over time), qualitative research, and clinical intervention trials.

## What This Means for Patients

This scoping review demonstrates that oral health and mental well-being are closely linked. For patients, families, and caregivers, the findings highlight that oral health challenges—such as untreated dental issues, dry mouth, or difficulty accessing dental care—can significantly affect daily comfort, emotional well-being, and social interactions.

Addressing these challenges requires recognizing oral health as an essential part of total health. The review underscores the importance of developing integrated, person-centered care strategies that can better support the oral health needs of individuals experiencing mental health conditions.
