For decades, dental professionals have advised people to brush their teeth for two minutes, twice a day. However, questions frequently arise about whether spending an extra minute—brushing for three minutes instead of two—yields cleaner teeth and better plaque control. 

A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated data from single-session toothbrushing experiments to determine whether three minutes of brushing provides a measurable advantage over the standard two-minute routine.

## How the Research Was Conducted

The researchers conducted a comprehensive literature search across three major scientific databases: MEDLINE-PubMed, Cochrane-CENTRAL, and Embase, capturing eligible studies published up to May 2025. The protocol was registered on the PROSPERO database under the identifier CRD42023445354.

To ensure rigorous evaluation, the investigators:

- Scored the methodological quality and risk of bias of each included study using the RoB 2 (Risk of Bias 2) assessment tool.
- Evaluated the certainty of the overall evidence using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) framework.
- Analyzed outcomes using difference of means or standardized mean differences to compare plaque levels.

The review examined two distinct operational settings: brushing performed by the participants themselves and brushing performed by a dental professional. Within these categories, the researchers analyzed data separately for manual toothbrushes and powered toothbrushes.

## What the Data Showed

From their database search, the researchers identified 6 unique eligible publications that together provided 20 distinct comparisons. 

When the data were synthesized in meta-analyses, some statistically significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) emerged in favor of three-minute brushing:

- **Participant manual brushing:** Participants brushing their own teeth with a manual toothbrush showed lower final plaque scores when brushing for three minutes compared to two minutes.
- **Professional powered brushing:** When a professional brushed using a powered toothbrush, three minutes also achieved statistically lower final plaque scores compared to two minutes.

## Statistical Difference vs. Clinical Impact

Although the analysis detected statistical differences in certain subgroups, the authors emphasized the difference between numerical significance and practical benefit. 

Based on the GRADE evaluation, the researchers concluded with moderate certainty that there is only a very small to no difference in plaque reduction when comparing three minutes to two minutes of brushing. Because the overall effect size was so small, the clinical relevance of that extra minute of brushing was judged to be negligible.

In plain terms, while measuring devices in a trial can detect minor variations in plaque scores after an extra minute of brushing, that tiny difference is unlikely to change a person's actual oral health outcome in daily practice.

## Genuine Limitations of the Evidence

When interpreting these findings, several limitations within the available evidence must be kept in mind:

- **Single-session focus:** The included studies evaluated single toothbrushing exercises rather than tracking participants over weeks, months, or years to observe long-term gum health or cavity rates.
- **Limited study pool:** Only 6 publications met the strict eligibility criteria for the review, providing 20 comparisons across various brushing methods and operator types.
- **Short-term plaque indices:** Plaque scores measured immediately after a single brushing event may not fully capture how plaque accumulates or changes over everyday routines.

## What This Means for Patients

This review provides reassuring evidence that the widely recommended two-minute brushing target remains well-founded. Extending brushing time to three minutes does not appear to provide a clinically meaningful reduction in dental plaque.

Rather than focusing on adding extra time beyond the recommended two minutes, patients can focus on reaching all tooth surfaces systematically with their manual or powered toothbrush during their regular two-minute routine.
