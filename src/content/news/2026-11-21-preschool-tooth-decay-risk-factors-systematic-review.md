Early childhood tooth decay remains a widespread health concern for young children. While many individual research papers have looked at how common cavities are among young children or focused on single habits, comprehensive research combining multiple risk factors across large populations of young children has been limited.

To get a clearer picture of tooth decay rates and contributing factors in children aged 36 to 71 months (roughly 3 to 5 years old), researchers conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis. A meta-analysis is a research method that combines and analyzes data from multiple independent studies to spot overarching trends. The researchers searched major scientific databases—including PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science—for observational studies published up through September 18, 2025.

## How the Analysis Was Conducted

The review compiled data from 29 observational studies involving a total of 26,563 preschool children. By combining these datasets, the researchers calculated the overall percentage of children affected by cavities and evaluated how specific dietary, oral hygiene, clinical, and age-related factors influenced decay risk.

Across all included studies, the combined average rate of tooth decay (known as pooled prevalence) was estimated at 46 percent. This indicates that nearly half of the preschool children evaluated across these studies had experienced cavities.

## Key Habits and Factors Linked to Higher Decay Risk

The researchers evaluated several habits and biological factors to determine how strongly they were connected to cavities. They measured these connections using odds ratios, which compare the likelihood of tooth decay occurring in the presence of a specific factor versus its absence:

- **Dental Plaque:** The presence of dental plaque—a sticky film of bacteria that coats teeth—had the strongest connection to decay. Children with plaque had 3.18 times higher odds of having cavities compared to those without.
- **Sugar Consumption:** General sugar intake was associated with 2.53 times higher odds of tooth decay.
- **Sugar-Sweetened Beverages:** Frequently drinking sugary beverages was linked to 2.37 times higher odds of cavities.
- **Older Age:** Within the 36 to 71 month age range, older children had 2.36 times higher odds of having tooth decay than younger toddlers.
- **Nocturnal Feeding:** Feeding children during the night was associated with 2.16 times higher odds of cavities.

## Protective Daily Practices

The analysis also identified two regular habits associated with lower odds of developing tooth decay:

- **Parental Supervision of Toothbrushing:** Children whose parents actively supervised their daily brushing had lower odds of decay (an odds ratio of 0.60, representing a 40 percent reduction in odds compared to unsupervised brushing).
- **Fluoridated Toothpaste:** Using toothpaste containing fluoride was also linked to lower decay risk (an odds ratio of 0.71, representing a 29 percent reduction in odds).

## Study Limitations to Keep in Mind

While the review provides valuable insights into early childhood oral health, the authors noted important limitations:

First, there was extremely high variation (heterogeneity) among the 29 included studies due to differences in geographical regions, study designs, and sample sizes. Because of this variability, the overall 46 percent decay figure must be interpreted with caution.

Second, because all included papers were observational studies, the findings demonstrate statistical associations rather than proven direct cause-and-effect relationships. As a result, the data cannot definitively prove that altering a specific habit will automatically prevent cavities, nor can it confirm the exact clinical effectiveness of preventive programs.

## What This Means for Patients and Parents

This broad review highlights that early childhood tooth decay affects a significant portion of preschool-aged children, but daily habits play a major role. Ensuring that young children brush with fluoridated toothpaste under adult supervision appears to offer meaningful protection against cavities. At the same time, managing plaque buildup, limiting sugar and sweet drinks, and avoiding nighttime feedings can help lower the risk of tooth decay during these early developmental years.
