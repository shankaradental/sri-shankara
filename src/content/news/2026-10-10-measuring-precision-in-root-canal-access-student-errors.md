## Understanding the First Step of Root Canal Treatment

When a tooth requires root canal treatment (endodontic therapy), the practitioner must first create an opening through the crown to reach the inner pulp chamber and root canals. This opening is known as an endodontic access cavity. Preparing an access cavity requires careful technique: the opening must provide clear access to locate and clean every canal, while conserving as much healthy tooth structure as possible.

A study conducted in a pre-clinical laboratory investigated how accurately fifth-semester dental students performed these traditional access preparations, evaluating the frequency of common errors and measuring the amount of tooth structure removed during the process.

## How the Study Was Conducted

The research evaluated a sample of 123 teeth treated in a pre-clinical endodontics laboratory setting. The researchers divided their assessment into two standardized parts:

1. **Visual and radiographic evaluation:** Standardized photographs and X-ray images (radiographs) were taken of each tooth. Two calibrated, independent evaluators used a questionnaire to assess access quality and document specific procedural errors.
2. **Measurement of tooth wear:** Using computational analysis with specialized imaging software (CTAn, version 1.16.1), the researchers measured the root canal area on radiographs before and after access preparation to determine how much tooth structure had been worn away.

All collected data were evaluated using statistical software (Jamovi, version 2.4.11) to carry out descriptive and inferential analyses.

## Error Rates and Patterns Identified

Out of the 123 teeth analyzed, only 31.7% of the accesses were completely error-free and classified as ideal. The remaining preparations showed one or more technical imperfections.

The most frequent issue identified was overextension, which refers to removing more tooth structure than necessary. Internal overextension was observed in 81.0% of teeth, while external overextension occurred in 47.6%. In addition, 19.0% of preparations showed a failure to completely remove the pulp chamber roof (the layer of dentin that covers the pulp space).

Other errors recorded by the evaluators included:

- **Underextension:** Where insufficient tooth structure was removed to provide adequate visibility, observed internally in 11.9% of teeth and externally in 13.1%.
- **Failure to identify canals:** In 9.5% of preparations, root canals were missed.
- **Gouging and thinning:** Excessive or uneven carving of internal tooth walls was detected in 8.3% of teeth.
- **Perforation:** An accidental puncture through the tooth structure (supracrestal perforation) occurred in 1.2% of cases.

When analyzing the amount of tooth wear, the researchers found a statistically significant difference based on tooth anatomy. Multi-rooted teeth, specifically molars, showed a higher average amount of tooth wear following access preparation compared to other teeth (p < 0.05).

## Study Limitations to Keep in Mind

When reviewing these findings, it is important to consider the context and limitations of the research:

- **Educational and laboratory setting:** The procedures were performed by fifth-semester undergraduate students during training in a simulated laboratory, not by licensed clinicians treating patients in a clinic.
- **Sample size:** The study was limited to 123 teeth from a single training cohort.
- **Strict assessment criteria:** The researchers noted that their evaluation criteria were extremely rigorous, which contributed to the high rate of recorded imperfections.

The authors concluded that the rate of imperfections highlights a clear need to improve pre-clinical training through updated curricula and more effective teaching methodologies.

## What This Means for Patients

For dental patients, this study provides insight into the complexity of root canal procedures and how dentists learn to perform them. Gaining access to root canals requires balancing adequate visibility with the preservation of natural tooth tissue. Rigorous laboratory studies like this help dental educators identify exactly where trainees encounter difficulties, supporting better training methods so that future dentists can perform procedures with greater precision.
