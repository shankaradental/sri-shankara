## Understanding Root Canal Cleaning and Post-Treatment Discomfort

When a tooth's internal nerve tissue dies—a condition known as tissue necrosis—a root canal treatment is performed to clean out bacteria and debris. In many instances, non-vital teeth cause no active pain beforehand; clinicians refer to these as asymptomatic necrotic permanent teeth. During single-visit nonsurgical root canal treatment, the dentist cleans and shapes the root canals inside the tooth in a single appointment.

One specific technique used during cleaning is called foraminal enlargement. The apical foramen is the natural opening at the tip of the tooth root where blood vessels and nerves enter. Foraminal enlargement involves intentionally widening this tiny opening to ensure thorough cleaning of the deepest portion of the canal. However, dental researchers have questioned whether widening this root tip causes increased pain after treatment. A systematic review and meta-analysis examined clinical trial data to compare postoperative pain levels in teeth treated with and without foraminal enlargement.

## How the Systematic Review Was Conducted

The research team followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews, registering their protocol in PROSPERO (CRD420250652786). They searched four major databases—MEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Central—up to May 20, 2025.

The team looked for randomized controlled trials comparing single-visit nonsurgical root canal therapy with foraminal enlargement against therapy without it in asymptomatic necrotic permanent teeth. The RoB 2 tool was used to assess risk of bias in the selected studies, and a random-effects model was used for statistical meta-analysis. In total, six randomized controlled trials involving 426 teeth met the inclusion criteria.

## What the Research Found

The combined statistical analysis showed that foraminal enlargement was associated with significantly greater postoperative pain compared to non-enlargement. The overall standardized mean difference—a measure used to combine results from different pain scales—was 1.02 (95% confidence interval: 0.24 to 1.80; p = 0.01).

Subgroup analyses demonstrated statistically significant increases in pain at specific time points, notably at 12 hours and 72 hours after the procedure. The researchers concluded that the increased discomfort associated with foraminal enlargement was transient, occurring primarily during the first week after treatment.

## Study Quality and Limitations

The review highlighted several important limitations that frame these findings:

- **Low Certainty:** The overall certainty of the evidence was categorized as low, largely due to inconsistency across the individual studies.
- **High Heterogeneity:** The statistical variation between study results was substantial, with an I² value of 92.3%. Excluding two influential studies reduced this variation without altering the overall direction of the effect.
- **Small Pool of Studies:** The meta-analysis relied on six studies containing a combined total of 426 teeth.

## What This Means for Patients

If you are scheduled for a root canal on a tooth that has lost vitality but is not currently causing pain, it is useful to know that temporary post-procedure discomfort can occur. Techniques aimed at cleaning the very end of the root canal tip may lead to a temporary increase in pain during the first few days, particularly around 12 to 72 hours post-treatment. This discomfort typically resolves within the first week. Discussing what to expect after your procedure with your dentist can help you prepare for recovery and manage any mild post-treatment sensitivity appropriately.
